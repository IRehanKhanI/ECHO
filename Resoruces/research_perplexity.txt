Earth Locations That Analog Permanent Moon Bases and Mars Missions

1. What is a terrestrial analog?
A terrestrial analog is an Earth location that reproduces one or more physical, geological, biological, or
operational conditions found on the Moon or Mars. No Earth location perfectly copies either world.
Instead, each site is selected for a particular purpose, such as testing rover movement, drilling, habitat
design, life-detection instruments, astronaut operations, or resource extraction.
NASA describes analog research as a way to test instruments, procedures, habitats, communications,
and human performance before conducting missions on the Moon or Mars.

3. Stronger Moon-versus-Mars comparison
Moon analogs
The Moon is characterized by:
Airless conditions
Very low gravity
Extreme temperature changes
Fine, abrasive regolith

Volcanic basalt and impact craters
Permanently shadowed polar regions
Potential water ice in polar cold traps
No natural atmosphere for protection or heat retention
The most useful Earth locations for lunar research are therefore volcanic, dusty, rocky, polar, or
resource-focused sites.
Best lunar analogs:
Mauna Kea: volcanic rocks and tephra can be used to test excavation and oxygen-extraction
systems.
Kilauea: basaltic lava flows and lava caves support rover navigation, mapping, and geological
fieldwork.
Black Point Lava Flow: suitable for testing rovers, habitats, autonomy, and difficult traverses.
Antarctic sites: useful for cold, isolation, habitat, and logistics testing, although they do not
reproduce the Moon’s vacuum or low gravity.
Arctic or high-altitude volcanic sites: useful for testing low-temperature equipment and
astronaut operations.

Mars analogs
Mars is characterized by:
A thin carbon-dioxide atmosphere
Cold temperatures
Dust and wind
Ancient rivers, lakes, and deltas
Impact craters
Basaltic volcanic rocks
Subsurface ice and possible brines
Strong isolation and communication delays
Potential ancient or surviving microbial environments
The most useful Earth locations for Mars research are therefore cold, dry, dusty, volcanic, chemically
unusual, isolated, or biologically sparse environments.
Best Mars analogs:
Atacama Desert: extremely dry and low in microbial abundance, making it valuable for lifedetection research.

Haughton Crater: combines impact geology, cold, isolation, and difficult logistics.
McMurdo Dry Valleys: among the closest Earth environments to Mars in terms of cold and
dryness.
MDRS in Utah: focuses on simulated habitat operations, EVA, field science, and crew
procedures.
Pilbara: useful for ancient geology, volcanism, mineralogy, and evidence of past habitability.
Negev Desert: useful for human exploration simulations and desert operations.
Pyhäsalmi Mine: useful for confined, underground, autonomous, and resource-extraction
scenarios.

4. Permanent-base suitability criteria
A good analog for a permanent lunar or Martian base should not be selected only because it looks
similar. It should also support long-duration testing.

Environmental criteria
Similar terrain type, such as basalt, crater deposits, loose regolith, ice, or dry sediment
Comparable surface roughness and slope
Similar temperature range or extreme thermal conditions
Similar dust, rock, and soil behavior
Availability of subsurface or surface ice analogs
Geological features that can be mapped and measured

Resource criteria
Presence of water, ice, minerals, or oxygen-bearing rocks
Possibility of testing in-situ resource utilization, or ISRU
Access to materials for construction or shielding
Ability to test excavation, drilling, and sample-processing equipment

Operational criteria
Isolation from roads, cities, and communication infrastructure
Difficulty of resupply
Terrain suitable for rover and walking trials
Ability to simulate communication delays
Safe areas for habitat deployment

Availability of emergency evacuation routes

Human and habitat criteria
Permission for multi-day or multi-week campaigns
Ability to test sleeping, food, sanitation, and medical procedures
Space for habitat and life-support equipment
Capacity to study crew workload, stress, isolation, and teamwork
Facilities for training and emergency response

Legal and environmental criteria
Possibility of obtaining research and land-use permits
Low risk of permanent ecological damage
Clear ownership or management authority
Protection of Indigenous, cultural, and scientific sites
Compliance with aviation, radio, drone, and environmental regulations

5. Important limitations

Limitation

Why it matters

Earth has a thick
atmosphere

Earth sites cannot perfectly reproduce the Moon’s vacuum or Mars’s thin
atmosphere.

Earth has higher gravity

Human movement, rover suspension, drilling, and soil behavior differ from lunar
and Martian conditions.

Earth has active biology

Even the driest deserts contain microorganisms, unlike the known lunar
environment.

Earth has liquid water and
weather

Rain, wind, erosion, and biological activity can change the terrain.

Earth sites are easier to
access

A remote Earth site still has more support than a Moon or Mars base.

Regulations differ by
location

National parks, Indigenous territories, border regions, and polar areas may
require significant permits.

No site matches every
condition

A reliable study should combine multiple analog sites instead of choosing one
“perfect” location.

The correct approach is to use a multi-site analog model. For example, Mauna Kea can represent
lunar regolith and ISRU, Atacama can represent extreme dryness and life detection, and Haughton
Crater can represent isolation, impact terrain, and field operations.

6. Recommended scoring model for the team’s app
The application can rank Earth locations using a weighted score:
Analog Score = 0.25G + 0.20T + 0.15R + 0.15O + 0.15H + 0.10L

Where:
G = geological similarity
T = thermal and climatic similarity
R = resource similarity
O = operational usefulness
H = habitat and human-testing suitability
L = legal and logistical feasibility

Each category can be scored from 0 to 100.

Example interpretation
Atacama: high climate and life-detection similarity, moderate logistics suitability
Mauna Kea: high geological and ISRU similarity, moderate climate similarity
Haughton Crater: high operational and terrain similarity, low resource similarity
McMurdo Dry Valleys: high cold and dryness similarity, difficult access
MDRS: high habitat and crew-operation suitability, lower geological similarity than volcanic sites
The app should display not only a percentage but also the reasons behind it:

Atacama — 85% Mars analog
High dryness, strong life-detection relevance, and useful terrain; lower score for temperature,
atmospheric pressure, and long-term logistics.
This makes the result more trustworthy than showing an unexplained “96.9% match.”

8. Final conclusion
No single Earth location perfectly represents the Moon or Mars. The strongest analog strategy
combines specialized sites:
Mauna Kea and Kilauea for lunar volcanic terrain, regolith, and ISRU
Atacama for Martian dryness and life-detection research
Haughton Crater for impact terrain, isolation, and field operations
McMurdo Dry Valleys for extreme cold, dryness, and drilling
MDRS and D-MARS for habitat, EVA, and crew procedures
Pilbara for ancient geology, volcanism, and past habitability

Pyhäsalmi Mine for confined environments, autonomy, and subsurface operations
The app’s main contribution should be to compare these sites transparently, explain why each site
matches, identify the limitations, and rank locations according to the specific mission objective. A
location that is excellent for rover testing may not be suitable for habitat testing or resource
extraction.
The NASA sources support the use of Atacama, Haughton Crater, Antarctic environments, Mauna Kea,
and volcanic Hawaii as planetary analogs. The human-performance review supports MDRS, D-MARS,
Lunares, Haughton, and HI-SEAS as facilities for habitat, EVA, isolation, communications, and crew
research.
